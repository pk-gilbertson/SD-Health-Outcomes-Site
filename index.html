Strongest year-to-year matches

How these are found

Each measure is turned into its percent change from the year before. Comparing changes rather than counts keeps population growth and aging from making every cause look related.

For each pair we measure how closely those changes line up (a correlation from −1 to 1), count the years both moved the same way, and repeat the comparison without 2020 and 2021 to see whether the pandemic years carry the pattern. Totals are never paired with their own causes.

With about ten yearly changes per measure, strong-looking matches happen by chance. Treat these as leads to check against rates, county data, or how deaths are coded. Moving together does not mean one causes the other.

Know what the numbers mean

This prototype reorganizes figures already published by the South Dakota Department of Health. It does not replace those reports, estimate anything, or contain record-level data.

Important distinctions

  • Counts, not rates: every measure here is a number of deaths. Rates per 100,000 residents will come once population figures are added. A rising count can come from a larger or older population.
  • All ages versus excluding infants: DOH’s Causes of Death tables leave out deaths of infants under one year old. Its Mortality Reports count all ages. The two are kept as separate series and never joined in one line.
  • Provisional: the latest year in a Mortality Report changes as records arrive. Provisional points are drawn hollow. Earlier releases are kept and shown as revisions.
  • Suppressed: values DOH hides to protect privacy stay hidden. They show as gaps, never zeros.
  • Withheld: values that fail a data check are left off the charts until confirmed. The measure’s panel explains which check failed.
  • Year-to-date tables: monthly reports show January to the current month. They are never treated as full-year totals.

How overlapping reports are reconciled

Data checks

Before a value appears, the pipeline checks each table’s own arithmetic and compares reports that cover the same years:

  • Each row’s yearly values add up to its printed total.
  • No single cause is larger than the table’s total deaths.
  • All-ages deaths minus deaths excluding infants gives a believable number of infant deaths.
  • A count excluding infants is never larger than the all-ages count.
  • Final figures don’t change between editions of the same report.

What would make this more useful next?

Population figures would turn counts into rates. County tables would allow maps and comparisons between places. Questions about whether programs work, or how health connects to education and employment, need record-level data shared under data use agreements. That is beyond what public reports can answer.
